How the number is produced

A dedicated eCash node runs with logtimemicros=1. Every transaction writes two lines to its log — one when it enters the mempool, one when Avalanche finalises it — and the time between them, to the microsecond, is the reading. There is no model and nothing to tune: it is a subtraction.

Once a day the readings for the previous UTC day are rolled into one row: count, p10, p50, p90, min, max. That row is never edited afterwards. The figure at the top of this page is the median of those daily medians, which is why a single busy hour cannot move it and why no particular day can be chosen to flatter it.

What we throw away

A negative interval — the finalise line older than the mempool line — is dropped rather than clamped. It means log lines arrived out of order or a clock stepped, and neither yields a real measurement.

An earlier version quietly synthesised sub-second values to hide the fact that the log only had second resolution. That was removed when microsecond logging was switched on, and the error it had been introducing — about 420 ms on average — is recorded in the source rather than forgotten.

The share of transactions final in under three seconds is computed only from histogram bins that sit entirely below three seconds. A bin straddling the line is excluded, so the published share is about 3% lower than the true one. The bias points away from us on purpose.

What this is not

Read this before citing

These are readings from one node, in one place on the network. A node elsewhere will see slightly different numbers, because part of what is measured is how fast news reaches this machine. It is an honest measurement of a real vantage point, not a network-wide average — and nobody, including us, can publish the latter from a single host.

Percentiles come from a fixed histogram, which resolves up to 60 seconds. When a percentile would land above that ceiling the page shows no number at all rather than the ceiling, because a saturated percentile reads like a healthy one. The count of readings past the ceiling is published alongside, so a silent percentile always has a visible reason.

Check it yourself

The series behind this chart is served raw at api.ecashlive.net/history — the same response this page reads, with no rounding applied on the way out. Recompute the median from it and you should land exactly on the figure above.
